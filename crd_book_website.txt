Preface
The book Suvarnna Rekha honors the long-standing custom of handloom
weaving in the lush and radiant village of Eravathody, which is tucked away in
the center of Thrissur, Kerala. This rooted community, which has been home to
generations of talented weavers, is still preserving the richness of a craft that
has been passed down through the generations as well as the rhythm of the
loom. The people of the area, their tales, and the ordinary beauty of the weaving process are all chronicled in this book. It embodies the spirit of a craft that
has influenced lives and identities, from the gentle clatter of wooden looms to
the threads dyed under the sun. This book is a reflection of a way of life woven
with tenderness, tenacity, and silent pride, rather than just a catalog of textiles.

Thrissur
Thrissur, meaning “small sacred place,” is known as the cultural capital of Kerala. Rich in heritage and tradition, it thrives as a centre of festivals, art, and classical performances. Around 90% of its people speak Malayalam, while the rest communicate in English or Tamil.Thrissur, in central
Kerala, lies between the Arabian Sea and the Western Ghats, with a landscape of coastal plains, rivers, and forests.The area has a tropical monsoon climate, and the Periyar and Bharathapuzha rivers flow close by.
Thrissur, formerly called Trichur, gets its name from Thirusshivaperoor, which
translates to “the sacred land of Lord Shiva” in Malayalam. Rich in history, it
played a crucial part in culture during its brief time as the capital of King Rama
Varma’s Kingdom of Kochi. A melting pot of international influence, Thrissur
attracted traders and tourists from distant civilizations like the Greeks, Romans,
Persians, and Arabs. Thrissur, Kerala’s Cultural Capital, is home to the stunning
Thrissur Pooram, a temple celebration renowned for its colorful customs and
lavishly decorated elephants. Kerala’s traditional art forms, including Kathakali,
Koodiyattam, and Mohiniyattam, have long flourished in the city, which has
produced generations of artists. Additionally, it is home to prestigious establishments that preserve and advance the state’s rich literary and artistic legacy,
such as the Kerala Sahitya Akademi and the Kerala Sangeetha Nataka Akademi.

Eravathody, a small village in Thiruvilwamala panchayat of Thrissur, Kerala, is known for its
deep-rooted handloom weaving tradition. The
name originates from Eravath Mana, an old ancestral home once located in the area. As a
weaving neighbourhood (thodi) formed around
it, the village came to be known as Eravathody.
Nearly 200 years ago, the Raja of Kochi brought
Mudaliar weaving families from Tamil Nadu to
craft fine Kasavu garments for the royal household. These families settled here and passed
down their skills through generations, blending Tam-

The Eravathody Handloom Weavers’ Co-operative Society was established on 5th July 1941.
Once 500 strong, it now has only 46 members
as many shifted to other livelihoods. The society,
which holds the Handloom Mark, owns 19 looms
and is known for its unique Kasavu products, experimenting with borders and colour combinations that set them apart from other clusters. The
society has received several accolades, including for weaving the highest number of uniforms
in the district. Notably, senior artisan Mr. Sundaram Mudali was honoured at the “Kalaparam Aaya
Kaithari” event for his intricate Theyyam-inspired
design woven into the mundani (pallu) of a saree.

Structure
There are four divisions within the society. The
first is the office, which also houses the secretary’s
workstation. The second part is used as a small
store or storage space where completed goods
are stored or offered for sale nearby. The last section, which is separated into two sections, one with
pit looms and the other with regular looms which is
devoted to the looms. A traditional setup that provides more control and ease for intricate work, pit
looms are constructed into shallow ground-level
pits that enable weavers to sit with their legs inside
while weaving. Since all four sections are a part
of a single, interconnected structure, moving between administrative, storage, and weaving tasks
is simple.

Weather
Challenges
The monsoon season presents a number of difficulties for craftspeople. Because of the increased
moisture in the air, wood swells, changing its dimensions and making precise work challenging
for those who work with it. The high humidity also
makes it difficult for handloom weavers because it
causes cotton threads to absorb moisture, which
makes the yarn heavy, damp, and difficult to work
with. Their work is further slowed down during this
season by the fact that water frequently seeps
through the society building’s roof and the damp
floors create unsafe and uncomfortable working
conditions.

The Loom
Handloom refers to a traditional method of weaving cloth manually on
looms that operate without electricity. This age-old craft has deep cultural roots and has been passed down through generations. Handloom weaving produces unique, superior textiles through expert craftsmanship. The process involves warping, setting the loom, and then
hand-weaving the fabric using natural fibers like cotton, silk, wool, and jute.
The subtle variations in texture, color, and pattern that are suggestive of handloom textiles’ handmade authenticity are what set them apart. Compared to
mechanized alternatives, the deliberate and slow process uses a lot less energy
and produces less waste. Future generations can benefit from the preservation
of traditional skills and cultural knowledge by supporting handloom artisans.
In Kerala, handloom cooperatives play a vital role in preserving these time-honoured techniques. These societies bring local weavers together to share resources, support each other, and market their creations collectively. Prominent
examples include the Balaramapuram Handloom Weavers’ Cooperative Society in Thiruvananthapuram, Kuthampully Society in Thrissur, Kudumbashree
handloom units across the state, Chendamangalam Society in Ernakulam,
Kasaragod Society, Kannur Society, and Chittur Society in Palakkad. These
collectives are essential in keeping Kerala’s rich weaving tradition alive while
providing livelihoods to thousands of artisans.

Types of Loom
The people of Eravathody Handloom Society use two main types of looms: the pit
loom and the frame loom. The pit loom is built into the ground, with its base and
pedals set inside a shallow pit. The weaver steps down into this space and works in
a semi-seated position, adjusting their feet on the pedals to operate the loom. This
traditional setup provides better control, especially for intricate weaving.

The frame loom, on the other hand, is constructed using wooden rods and
panels fixed at right angles, forming a box-like structure. It is easier to manage
and offers portability, as it can be dismantled and reassembled as needed.
In Eravathody, these looms come in various types based on the width of fabric they
can produce. This allows the weavers to create a wide range of textiles—from
narrow borders to broader pieces—helping the society cater to different market demands and maintain a diverse product line.

Raw Materials
and Sourcing
The main raw material used in weaving at Eravathody is cotton yarn.
The society office typically purchases this yarn in bulk from Salem,
Tamil Nadu, at mill gate prices. After delivery, it is separated into
hanks and given to the weavers after being stored until needed. As
an alternative, some weavers decide to purchase the yarn (known
locally as paavu) directly from Jayalakshmi Textiles in Salem; however, this prevents them from being eligible for government subsidies.
The cotton yarn is spun into bobbins using a traditional charkha and
then soaked in rice starch to make it stronger and easier to weave.

Eravathody also uses zari yarn, which is frequently connected to Kasavu products. The society uses gold-painted
polyester yarn, which is sourced from Surat, as a less expensive alternative to the traditional zari, which is made of

frequently more expensive and used less frequently.
The society typically works with yarns in their natural
shades because they don’t have an in-house dyeing unit.
Vibrant, colorful yarns are rarely used in their designs; instead, they are reserved for special detailing or borders
(kara). Small quantities of dyed yarn are imported or purchased locally when necessary from Selvaraj & Co., one
of their main suppliers, in Coimbatore. While Selvaraj & Co.
also provides weaving tools and accessories, local vendors
supply the wax used to polish Kasavu. Repairs are made
locally in the event of damage, and the society maintains
meticulous purchase records to guarantee constant quality.

Yarn Spooling
The process of winding involves moving yarn from a hank onto a bobbin, typically with the aid of a charkha. A thread is carefully wound around
the bobbin after the hank has been mounted onto a winding wheel. bobbin winding, in which yarn is wound onto a larger bobbin for warping, and
pirn winding, in which yarn is wound onto a small pirn to be used in the
shuttle for wefting, are the two primary forms of winding. This task is traditionally performed by women in the home using charkhas, though
some use mechanized winding machines for increased efficiency.
A charka is a basic spinning wheel that is controlled by hand using a spindle,
which is a vertical lever. The cotton fibers are pulled out while the spindle
rotates and wound into a slender thread. To create a continuous yarn thread,
the flyer directs the twisted fiber onto the bobbin. In mechanized bobbin
winding, yarn is drawn from a supply by a motor-driven spindle, which then
rapidly winds it uniformly onto a bobbin.

Beaming
A minimum of five workers are needed
to manage the precise and continuous
process of winding the entire set of warp
yarns onto a single beam. To ensure
even tension, the yarn must be properly
combed and kept untangled as it wraps
around the warp beam. Throughout this
process, a layer of plastic or newspaper

is always wrapped around the beam to
protect the yarn. This serves as a barrier
to lessen friction between the yarn and
the beam, preventing damage and enabling a more smooth effective weaving
experience, ultimately maintaining the
fabric’s quality and the loom’s functionality.

Warp Setup
Drafting is the process of threading
the warp through the heddles after the
warp beam is prepared. To accomplish this, two people must cooperate:
one carefully pulls each thread through
from the other side while the other
pushes it through the tiny hole in the
heald. In order to create the desired
weaving pattern, this step is essential.
Denting starts after drafting. Depending
on the fabric design, the warp threads are

inserted in a particular arrangement into
the reed’s dents or spaces. Generally, depending on the pattern specifications, two
to three threads may be inserted per dent.
Tie-up is the last phase of setup. The
treadles and heddles are attached in a
specific order in this step. The first treadle and the second heel, for instance,
might be connected. The complexity of
the weave being created defines how
many treadles and heddles are used.

Weaving
In the intricate and time-consuming process of handloom weaving, two sets of yarn are carefully interlaced at right angles to create fabric. The warp, or vertical threads, are tightly stretched across the loom
to create the cloth’s foundation. To create the surface, pattern, and feel of the fabric, the weft threads are
woven horizontally over and under the warp. The design progressively takes shape with each weft pass,
showcasing the weaver’s skill and attention to detail. Because the placement and combination of threads
affect the final textile’s appearance and function, the process combines skilled craftsmanship with artistic
sensibility.
Step 1: Setting the Loom
This begins by attaching the new warp threads to the remaining ends of the previous set. The threads
are then carefully detangled and combed through to ensure smooth movement.
Step 2: Forming the Shed
Shed formation involves lifting and lowering alternate sets of warp threads using the heddles, creating a
space for the weft to pass through.
Step 3: Passing the Weft
Also known as picking, this step involves moving the shuttle carrying the weft yarn across the shed, from
one side to the other.
Step 4: Tightening the Weft
In this stage, the inserted weft yarn is firmly pushed into place at the fabric’s edge, known as the fell,
ensuring a compact and even weave.

55

Step 5: Rolling the Cloth
Once a section of fabric is woven, it is wound onto the cloth roller in a process known as fabric winding
or take-up
.
Step 6: Feeding the Warp
This step regulates the release of warp yarn from the warp beam, ensuring the right amount of thread is
available for continuous weaving under steady tension.
Step 7: Treadle Coordination
The treadles control which warp threads are lifted. The weaver operates them in a repeated rhythm,
helping maintain the weaving pattern
.
Step 8: Joining Broken Threads
When a warp thread snaps, the weaver ties the broken ends using a special knot technique known as
the weaver’s knot to restore continuity in weaving.

Producing and promoting traditional handloom
goods is the mission of the Kerala based Eravathody Handloom Weavers Society. The society mostly uses retail locations, but it also accepts
large orders for festivals and special occasions.
Maintaining a robust network of wholesalers, they
consistently provide reputable establishments
and shops like Hantex. At trade shows and exhibits, they also participate. When the organization gives discounts of up to 20 percent to stimulate purchases and draw in a larger audience,
sales often surge around big festival seasons like
Onam. The supply chain in the society is efficient.
The textiles are made by hand by local weavers
and are sent to the society headquarters for processing and shipping.

Quality standards
Every fabric produced in Eravathody is made entirely by hand, a tradition that defines the cluster’s
identity. Eravathody thrives only on the strength of
traditional handloom weaving. The soul that goes
into each thread, in addition to the technique, is what
makes handloom unique. Every step, from spinning
to weaving, requires human hands and sincere care.
In addition to its emotional value, handloomed
cotton fabric has health advantages of its own.
The cloth breathes better thanks to the softer
weave produced by the manual process. Because of this, the fabric is naturally airy, kind
to the skin, and perfect for year-round com-

fort, particularly in India’s changing climate.
Although making a handloom item can take
several days, the finished product is nothing short of a wearable narrative. Every thread
carries the patience and artistry of the weavers at the society. It’s more than just apparel; it’s
a subdued expression of the makers’ concern.
Textiles produced by machines might be faster
and less expensive, handlooms offer something
far more valuable: the human touch. Even though
the worth of such craftsmanship is hard to quantify, one glance and one touch are enough to show
why it is worth a little more.

Government schemes
1. Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana
(PMJJBY)
This life insurance scheme ensures financial protection for weavers and their families. By paying a
small annual premium, they receive life coverage,
offering both peace of mind and long-term security to those dependent on weaving incomes.

4. Weaving Accessories Grant
Every three years, eligible weavers receive
₹3,000 to invest in essential tools such as looms
and shuttles. This scheme eases the financial
burden of maintaining equipment and helps sustain the quality of their craft.

2. Workshed Grant Scheme
This grant helps artisans improve their working conditions by providing financial assistance
for constructing or renovating worksheds. A
well-ventilated, safe, and comfortable space enhances both the quality of life and work productivity for weavers.

5. Contributory Thrift Fund (CTF)
This scheme promotes financial savings by
deducting 8% from a weaver’s earnings and
matching it with an equal contribution from the
government. Over time, it builds a strong savings
base, offering long-term financial security and
supporting future needs or emergencies.

3. Production Incentive Scheme
To encourage higher output, this initiative provides up to ₹1,000 as a reward for meeting specific production targets. It helps artisans manage
production expenses while motivating growth and
efficiency in their work.

6. Free Handloom School Uniform Scheme
This initiative provides hand-woven uniforms to
schoolchildren, creating consistent and largescale demand for handloom products. It offers
stable income opportunities for weavers while
increasing the visibility of traditional textile crafts.
