‘Weaver’s World’

Preface
‘Suthanthira Paravaikal’ is an ode to the practice of weaving, handloom traditions, and the legacy
carried forward by the weaving families of Eravathody, a quiet village in the Thiruvilwamala panchayat of
Kerala’s Thrissur district.
Through the documentation of ordinary moments spent at the loom and the communal spirit that unites
the people, the book delves into the rhythm of life in this weaving village. It displays all aspects of the
weaving process, from the warp’s setting to the fabric’s finishing, as well as the several traditional tools
and methods the weavers employ. In their world, where craft and culture coexist together, rich images,
portraits, and process documentation provide a glimpse.
‘Suthanthira Paravaikal’ serves as a visual and narrative platform for the community to present
their heritage with pride. It reflects their silent dedication, the quality of their craftsmanship, and
their continued relevance in a fast-changing world — proving that tradition, when held with care, can
indeed take flight.

Introduction

Thiruvilwamala
Thiruvilwamala, a place surrounded by temples and the cries of peacocks, set along the banks of the
Bharathapuzha River also known as Nila. This charming temple town, tucked away in Kerala’s northern region,
is well-known for its deep cultural heritage and history. The Mudaliar community, whose ancestors migrated
from Tamil Nadu almost 200 years ago, is particularly well-known for its weaving customs, which have made
Thiruvilwamala famous.
Thiruvilwamala is home to some of Kerala’s most revered spiritual sites. The Thiruvilwamala Sree Vilwadrinatha
Temple, said to have emerged from a cave where a golden Vilwa tree grew, stands as its most iconic. Other
major attractions include the Shree Rama Temple, Punarjani Caves—believed to cleanse sins for those who pass
through—and Ivor Madom, a sacred site tied to the Pandavas and rituals for the departed.
Thiruvilwamala experiences a tropical, humid climate with abundant, heavy rainfall. Summer sets in by March
and stretches through May, gradually blending into the intense downpours of the southwest monsoon from June
to September. During these months, the skies remain overcast, and the landscape turns lush and vibrant. As
October approaches, the rains slowly retreat, making way for a more serene post-monsoon season. Throughout
the year, Thiruvilwamala enjoys refreshing seasonal rains and a gentle, cool atmosphere that adds to its charm.

The Arrival
of Mudaliyar

A few centuries ago, during a time of flourishing art
and craftsmanship, the Raja of Kochi sought to elevate
his court’s prestige by commissioning garments of
unmatched quality. To achieve this, he invited skilled
weaving families from Tamil Nadu—renowned for their
mastery in fine handloom techniques—to settle in his
kingdom and create textiles exclusively for the royal
family.
These artisans were warmly welcomed and granted
land near the Eravath Mana, a culturally significant
Namboothiri household. More precisely, they were
settled in the Thodi of Eravath Mana—a section of
the estate well-suited for their work, offering both
proximity to the court and a peaceful environment for
weaving.
Over time, many of these families chose to remain in
Kerala rather than return to Tamil Nadu, and the area
came to be known as Eravathody, evolving from its
original link to Eravath Mana. They established deep
roots, passing their craft through generations and
transforming what began as royal service into a lasting
cultural tradition.
Thus emerged a unique weaving village in Kerala—
where Tamil handloom traditions blended with the
local ethos, creating a legacy of craftsmanship that
continues to thrive.

Overview of

Eravathody Weavers
Formed on 5th July 1941, the Eravathody Handloom Weaver’s Co-operative Society has stood the
test of time as a proud centre of traditional weaving. Just a month after its inauguration, production
began in full swing with nearly 500 skilled artisans—mainly from the Tamil-origin Mudaliar
community—who brought with them a deep-rooted knowledge of handloom craft. This marked
the beginning of a committed weaving community bound by tradition, technique, and pride.
Eravathody quickly gained prominence for its quality and authenticity, earning the prestigious
Handloom Mark, which guarantees that the fabric is genuinely handwoven. Among its many
talented artisans, Mr. Sundaran Mudali received special recognition for his intricate weaving of
a Theyyam design on the pallu (mundani) of a saree—a remarkable piece that reflected Kerala’s
cultural richness in cloth, and was honoured at the Kalaparamaya Kaithari showcase.

The Sacred Duty

Among the Mudaliyar Tamil weavers, there was a strong belief passed
down through generations: their community was made to weave cloths
for gods. This idea shaped not only their occupation but their identity.
Weaving was not seen as mere labor—it was understood as a form of
devotion.
Over time, their religious practices reflected this belief. They became
devoted worshippers of multiple gods. Today, their traditions continue,
and many families from this community still uphold their faith through
regular worship.
Among the various gods they revere, Mariyamman stands out as the
most prominent. She is deeply respected and widely worshipped within
the community, and her presence plays a central role in their spiritual
lives.

Weather
Challenges
The weavers at Eravathody Handloom
Society face significant challenges during the
monsoon season. One of the major issues is
roof leakage—during heavy rains, water seeps
through the roof, often pooling on the floor.
To manage this, the weavers are forced to
cover the looms and other equipment with
tarpaulin sheets and plastic covers. Despite
these efforts, wooden looms suffer damage
due to prolonged exposure to dampness. Over
time, parts of the looms become infested with
wood-boring insects or fungus, which leads to
the gradual deterioration of the wood. These
damaged parts often require frequent repairs
or replacements.
The storage rooms are small and poorly
ventilated, making it difficult to store large
quantities of finished products. As a result, the
sarees and double munds absorb moisture,
risking damage or mould growth. To prevent
this, the weavers have to take the textiles
outside and expose them to sunlight regularly
to remove trapped moisture and preserve
fabric quality.

Raw Materials
Cotton yarn is the main material used in the production of Eravathody products. It is brought from Salem
and Coimbatore in bundles and wound into bobbins using the charkha. Sometimes, dyed cotton yarns are
used instead of zari or a mix of zari and dyed yarn to make different borders in saree.After winding, the
bobbins are soaked in starch (rice water) so that the threads stick to each other and the yarn becomes
stronger, which helps in increasing the overall strength of the fabric.
Zari is traditionally made of silver-wrapped silk yarn that is electroplated with gold. However, in Eravathody,
a more affordable alternative is used—gold-painted polyester yarn. This zari yarn is sourced from Surat.
Once the yarn is wound, it is soaked in water to prevent it from getting reddish and unravelling during
weaving. Zari is more difficult to handle compared to cotton thread, as it tangles easily. Due to this and its
decorative nature, it is also more expensive.
The wax used to polish the kasavu is sourced from local suppliers. As for the weaving tools and accessories,
they are carefully crafted by skilled local aasharies (carpenters) who understand the specific needs of the
weavers. If any of the tools get damaged, they’re usually repaired by the aasharies

Winding Zari on the Charkha

Winding White Yarn onto the Bobbin

Zari yarn is first drawn from a hank and wound
onto a small spool using a charkha. This step
requires precision, as zari threads are delicate
and prone to tangling. The charkha ensures
smooth and even winding, preparing the zari
for its use in intricate border weaving.

The zari is then transferred to a pirn, a slender
cone-shaped spool that fits snugly inside the
shuttle. Once loaded, the shuttle is placed on
the loom for weaving. This is especially used
during border weaving, where zari becomes
the weft thread that adds shimmer and
elegance to the fabric.

Alongside zari preparation, white cotton yarn
is wound onto bobbins using a simple winding
frame or tool. The cotton yarn is stronger and
used for the body of the fabric. These bobbins
are later inserted into the loom for weft
insertion or used during the warping process,
depending on the design being woven.

Setting Up the Warp Distance

In the first step, two strong wooden stands are
tied using a rope to a fixed hook that is halfburied in the road. This hook is a permanent
fixture kept specifically for warping purposes.
The two stands are placed about 37 metres
apart, marking the required length for the
warp. This entire process uses the full stretch
of the street.

Yarn from the hedge-like creel is unravelled
and pulled carefully across the road to the
warp roller. Once fully extended, it is attached
to the roller at the opposite end. A comb
and the edge of the warp roller are used to
separate and align the fibres neatly, ensuring
smoothness and evenness in the yarn
distribution.

To maintain equal spacing between threads,
long wooden sticks are inserted at regular
intervals between the yarns. These sticks act
as spacers, making the rolling process more
controlled and uniform. They help prevent
tangling and support even winding as the
warp builds up layer by layer on the roller.

Sectioning the Warp for Fabric Length

Protecting and Rolling the Warp

Finishing and Carrying the Warp Beam

A rope of predetermined length is used to
divide the yarn into equal sections based
on the required fabric length—such as for
mundus or sarees. This ensures that each roll
of warp corresponds precisely to one garment
unit. Once sectioned, the actual rolling of the
yarn onto the beam begins, handled carefully
by the team.

Before continuing, protective materials like
newspapers are placed over the warp to
shield it from dirt or moisture during rolling. As
the yarn is wound, the wooden sticks placed
earlier are gradually removed. Workers move
closer to the roller as the yarn shortens, ensuring that tension remains even and the roll
stays neat and tight.

Once the entire yarn is rolled, the sticks are
removed, and the warp beam is fully ready.
The entire setup—stands, ropes, and tools—is
dismantled. The prepared warp roller is then
carried back to the weaving cluster. This
outdoor process, typically done in the middle
of the road, requires at least 7 skilled people to
complete efficiently.

Weaving

After warping is complete, the warp roller is
fixed securely onto the loom. The shuttle is
loaded with bobbins containing weft yarn and
placed in position. Zari threads, if required,
are inserted separately using a pulley system.
All components are checked and aligned to
ensure the loom is ready for smooth weaving.

In this step, the heddles are connected to
the treadles using ropes, enabling movement
during weaving. The warp threads are passed
through the reed, which spaces them evenly.
Finally, the threads are tied tightly to the padamaram (cloth beam), completing the loom
setup and allowing the threads to be lifted
and lowered during weaving.

Weaving begins by pressing the treadle on the
side where the shuttle is placed. This action
lifts specific warp threads, creating a shed for
the shuttle to pass through. A rope is pulled
to send the shuttle across, carrying the weft
yarn. The reed is used to beat each new weft
line into place, forming the fabric.

Border Weaving

Completion

Rolling the Fabric

As the fabric nears completion, the plain
cotton weft yarn is replaced with zari yarn
to form the border. This is known as kasavu
weaving. The zari is interwoven widthwise to
create the decorative golden border that is
typical in traditional Kerala textiles like mundu
and set sarees.

Once a full set is woven, a small gap is left
before starting the next set. When the cloth
beam is full, the fabric is cut and separated
using a blade. The pieces are folded and
neatly stacked. This process is repeated until
the warp yarn on the roller runs out and a new
warping cycle begins.

As the fabric grows, a rope is pulled to release
more yarn from the warp roller. The woven
fabric is then wound tightly onto the cloth
roller. The heddles are gently pushed back
to increase the weaving space, ensuring
the weaver can continue working without
obstruction as more fabric is created.

Marketing

Marketing
Eravathody Handloom Weavers Society is
a Kerala-based organization dedicated to
the production and promotion of traditional
handloom products. The society operates
primarily through retail outlets and also caters
to bulk orders for specific events and festivals.
They maintain a network with the wholesalers,
regularly supplying to reputed institutions and
retailers such as Hantex.
They also participate in exhibitions and
trade fairs. Sales usually peak during the
major festive seasons like Onam, where the
society offers discounts of up to 20% to boost
purchases and engage to a wider audience.
The society operates an efficient supply. Local
weavers handcraft the textiles and deliver
them to the society office, where they receive
direct payment. Each product is carefully
catalogued and stored, with a handloom tag
to ensure its authenticity. A quality check is
then carried out by the society secretary to
maintain consistent product standards.
The society sells through multiple channels.
Government bodies like Hantex may procure
goods directly. Products are also distributed
to various businesses in the neighboring
handloom hub of Kuthampully. Individual
customers can also make direct purchases.

Government Schemes
Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima
Yojana (PMJJBY): This life insurance
scheme offers financial security to weavers
and their families. For a very nominal annual
premium, it provides life coverage, ensuring
that a weaver’s dependents are protected in
case of an untimely death. It brings not just
peace of mind, but also a sense of dignity
and safety to families relying on handloom
incomes.
Workshed Grant: The Workshed Grant
aims to improve the working conditions
of weavers by providing financial aid for
building or renovating their worksheds. A safe,
functional, and well-ventilated workspace
helps in improving productivity and the quality
of life for artisans.

Production Incentive Scheme: This
scheme offers a performance-based reward
to boost productivity. Weavers who meet
specific production targets can earn up
to Rs 1,000 as an incentive. It’s a practical
way to encourage higher output by helping
artisans manage their production costs while
supporting the growth of their businesses.
Weaving Accessories Grant: Once every
three years, weavers receive Rs. 3,000 under
this scheme to purchase essential tools and
accessories, such as looms, shuttles, and other
necessary equipment. This support eases the
financial burden of maintaining their craft and
ensures they can continue producing highquality handloom products without struggle.

48 | MARKETING

Contributory Thrift Fund (CTF): The
CTF scheme promotes long-term financial
security for weavers. It works by deducting 8%
of the weaver’s wages and depositing it into
a personal savings account. The government
then contributes an equal 8%, effectively
doubling their savings. Over time, this builds
a solid foundation that can support future
needs, investments, or emergencies, giving
weavers greater financial independence.
Free Handloom School Uniform Scheme:
This initiative supplies schoolchildren with
uniforms made from hand-woven fabrics.
It provides steady, large-scale demand for
handloom products. For weavers, it ensures
consistent income and visibility for their craft.

51 | CONCLUSION

Conclusion
Eravathody stands as a living archive of tradition, craftsmanship, and quiet resilience.
Rooted in history yet Struggling with the demands of a modern world, the village
and its weavers reflect both the richness and the vulnerability of India’s handloom
heritage. Despite monsoon challenges, ageing artisans, and the lure of faster, cheaper
machine-made alternatives, the community continues to weave stories into threads—
each saree, mundu, and fabric carrying generations of knowledge, pride, and culture.
Suthanthira Paravaikal is more than just a book—it is a tribute to the freedom
of expression through craft, and to the hands that shape culture through quiet,
rhythmic labour. As the world races ahead, places like Eravathody remind us of
the beauty in slowing down, in choosing handmade over mass-produced, and
in valuing traditions that are not just historical, but still alive and meaningful.
To preserve this legacy, there is an urgent need for renewed interest, digital inclusion,
youth engagement, and infrastructural support. Only then can the “free birds” of
Eravathody continue to soar—unbound by time, yet deeply rooted in tradition.

Suthanthira Paravaigal is a heartfelt tribute to the unseen strength,
dignity, and quiet freedom of Eravathody’s handloom weavers.
Through woven textures, whispered memories, and everyday
resilience, this book gently unravels the lives of those who choose the
loom not just as a means of living, but as a symbol of independence.
Blending research, narrative, and visual documentation, this work delves into
the rhythms of daily life in weaving communities, the challenges they face, and
the quiet pride they carry. Each page reveals how the loom becomes a space
of autonomy, where time, effort, and tradition are woven into every fabric.
A valuable resource for designers, scholars, and anyone interested in craft,
culture, and community, Suthanthira Paravaigal celebrates the enduring
spirit of those who continue to choose handloom in a rapidly changing world.
